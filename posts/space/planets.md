We have sent robots to our Moon, Mars, Venus, and even an asteroid. And now we are planning to send one to Titan (a moon in Saturn) in a few years[^2]. But there is still a lot to explore out there, so let's think the following fun idea:

> Say we have an "invincible rover", meaning a robot that can explore a planet or moon while withstanding any pressure, radiation, heat (or lack of), and environment. Where should we drop it?

First, let's consider the candidates:

Our solar system has one star (the Sun), 8 planets and 5 dwarf planets[^1]. From closest to furthest from the Sun, we have:
- Mercury
- Venus
- Earth
- Mars
- Jupiter
- Saturn
- Uranus
- Neptune

And for dwarf planets, we have:
- Ceres
- Pluto
- Haumea
- Makemake
- Eris

Regarding the moons and asteroids, perhaps there are too many to list them here. Consider the bigger and perhaps most relevant ones[^3][^4]:

| Planet | Moons |
|--------|-------|
| Mercury | None |
| Venus | None |
| Earth | Moon (the one we all know) |
| Mars[^5] | Phobos, Deimos |
| Jupiter[^6] | 95, mainly Ganymede, Callisto, Io, Europa |
| Saturn[^7] | 274, mainly Titan, Enceladus, Hyperion, Mimas, Dione, Iapetus |
| Uranus[^8] | 28, mainly Miranda, Ariel, Umbriel, Titania, Oberon |
| Neptune[^9] | 16, mainly Triton, Despina |
| Ceres[^20] | None |
| Pluto[^10] | 5: Charon, Nix, Hydra, Kerberos, Styx |
| Haumea[^11] | Namaka, Hi'iaka |
| Makemake[^12] | "MK 2" |
| Eris[^13] | Dysnomia |

To select between these options, I will apply the following criteria:
1. Discard any of the planets/moons where we have too little data to work on a rover to explore it
2. Discard any place we have sent rovers already to
3. Discard any place where a space agency has a concept design of a rover to visit
4. The surface of the planet/moon must include solid matter for the rover to land

With the above criteria, the following destinations are discarded:
1. Pluto and moons, Haumea and moons, Makemake and MK 2, Eris and Dysnomia, moons in Uranus, moons in Neptune
2. Mars, our Moon
3. Titan[^2], Venus[^19], Phobos and Deimos[^14], Europa[^15], Enceladus[^16]
4. Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune

Our updated list of options after filtering is:
- Mercury
- Ceres
- Ganymede
- Callisto
- Io
- Hyperion
- Mimas
- Dione
- Iapetus

Each one of these candidates are quite interesting, but the one that stands out for me is Mercury, the only planet in the inner solar system we haven't even conceptualized an explorer robot to visit. I will dedicate a separate post on why choose Mercury. For now, let's talk about the other options and why or why not go there:

## Ceres

Ceres is a dwarf planet sitting between Mars and Jupiter[^20], and was visited by the spacecraft Dawn[^21] in 2015[^22].

### What we know[^22]

- Ceres is the largest object in the asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter, accounting for roughly 35% of the total mass in the asteroid belt
- Ceres is the only dwarf planet in the inner solar system
- Ceres contains a lot of water ice. We estimate the whole planet to be 25% ice
- Ceres is geologically active, holding cryovolcanoes that spit water into the surface
- Ceres has some bright spots (see [here](https://assets.science.nasa.gov/dynamicimage/assets/science/psd/solar/internal_resources/2948/Image_of_Ceres_with_bright_spot.jpeg?w=640&h=350&fit=clip&crop=faces%2Cfocalpoint)), which are salt deposits left from water that came up the surface and later evaporated
- Ceres has organic matter including large amounts of carbon material[^23], particularly in the craters Ernutet and Occator
- Ceres has "ammonia-rich clays"[^24]

### What questions remain

1. Where did Ceres form?
    - For objects formed within our solar system, the presence of ammonia can only happen if they originated far from the Sun, beyond the current average distance of Neptune
    - The clays with ammonia in Ceres leads to theories that it did not form in the asteroid belt where it is now, but is rather a TNO (trans-Neptunian object) or received materials from a TNO[^24]

2. Is Ceres habitable?
    - Ceres has lots of water, and active cryovolcanoes may hide liquid water from the surface
    - If large amounts of liquid brine exist, Ceres could potentially be a target for a space colony in the long-term
